Iron studies & Ferritin

Monday tips, 12 Oct 2026. Always tired or pale? What iron studies and ferritin show, who may need them and how to read the report.

What they are
A group of blood tests: serum iron (iron in the blood right now), TIBC / transferrin (the protein 'taxis' that carry iron), transferrin saturation (how full those taxis are) and ferritin (iron stored in the body). Iron is needed to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen.
Why it matters
In India's NFHS-5 survey (2019–21), about 57% of women aged 15–49 and about 67% of children aged 6–59 months had anaemia; low iron is one common cause. Signs include tiredness, weakness, pale skin, breathlessness, fast heartbeat, headache, cold hands and feet, brittle nails and cravings for ice or clay.
Who may need it
As your doctor advises: anaemia or small red cells on a CBC, heavy periods, pregnancy, poor diet (some children and vegetarians), frequent blood donors, stomach or gut disease, and people who get many blood transfusions (e.g. thalassaemia), where MRI may also be used to check liver and heart iron.
Preparation
Serum iron is highest in the morning and changes through the day, so a morning sample is better; many labs ask for about 8 hours of fasting. Ferritin alone usually needs no fasting. Follow your lab's instructions, and if you take iron tablets or injections, tell your doctor before the test.
Reading the result
Low iron usually shows low ferritin, low serum iron, high TIBC and low saturation; iron overload shows high ferritin and high saturation. WHO: in healthy people aged 5+, ferritin below about 15 ng/mL means iron deficiency. Transferrin saturation is approximately 20–50% in men and 15–45% in women; ranges vary by lab. Infection, inflammation or liver disease can raise ferritin, so it may look normal even when iron is low. Not every anaemia is low iron (thalassaemia trait also gives small red cells and does not need iron), and too much iron can harm organs: take iron only as your doctor advises.
