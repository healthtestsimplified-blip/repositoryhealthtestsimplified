Calcium & Phosphorus tests

Tuesday tips, 13 Oct 2026. Cramps, tingling or kidney stones? What calcium and phosphorus tests show, who may need them and how to read the report.

What they are
Blood tests for two bone minerals. About 98–99% of the body's calcium and about 85% of its phosphorus are stored in bones and teeth; the small amount in blood is vital for muscles and nerves. Parathyroid hormone, vitamin D and the kidneys keep the levels in balance, and when calcium goes up phosphorus often goes down, so they are usually tested together.
Signs of imbalance
Low calcium may cause tingling in the fingers, toes or around the lips, muscle cramps and twitching. High calcium often causes no symptoms at first; later there may be thirst, passing urine often, tiredness, constipation, nausea and kidney stones. These signs have many causes, so only a test can tell.
Common causes
High calcium: most often an overactive parathyroid gland or some cancers. Low calcium: low vitamin D, underactive parathyroid, kidney disease, low albumin or low magnesium. High phosphorus: often a kidney problem. Low phosphorus: low vitamin D, very poor nutrition, alcohol or an overactive parathyroid. Your doctor finds the cause, often with PTH and vitamin D tests.
Who may need it
As your doctor advises: routine check-ups (often part of a blood panel), cramps or tingling, kidney disease, parathyroid or bone problems, repeated kidney stones (urine calcium-creatinine ratio or 24-hour urine calcium), and people on medicines that affect calcium. A calcium test alone usually needs no special preparation; with other tests a few hours of fasting may be asked. Tell your doctor about calcium, vitamin D, antacids, water pills or thyroid medicine, and never stop a medicine on your own.
Reading the result
Approximate adult ranges: total calcium 8.5–10.2 mg/dL, ionised calcium 4.5–5.6 mg/dL, phosphorus 2.8–4.5 mg/dL (higher in children because bones are growing). Ranges vary by lab. About half of blood calcium is bound to albumin, so low albumin can make total calcium look low; the doctor may order ionised calcium. Normal blood calcium does not mean strong bones: the body takes calcium from bones to keep the blood level steady, and bone strength is checked with a bone density (DEXA) scan. A 'calcium score' is a CT scan of the heart arteries, not a blood test.
