ANA test by IFA (antinuclear antibody)

What antinuclear antibodies are, how the HEp-2 immunofluorescence (IFA) test works, what titer and pattern mean, when doctors order it, and why a positive ANA is not always a disease.

What is ANA?
Antibodies the immune system mistakenly makes against the nucleus of our own cells. Found in autoimmune diseases such as lupus (SLE), Sjögren's syndrome and scleroderma.
How IFA works
Patient serum is placed on a slide of HEp-2 cells; ANA binds the nucleus; a fluorescent-tagged antibody is added and the nucleus glows green under a fluorescence microscope. The American College of Rheumatology considers HEp-2 IFA the gold standard.
Titer & pattern
Serum is diluted (1:40, 1:80, 1:160...). The highest dilution that still glows is the titer; higher titer = higher chance of disease; cut-off differs by lab. Patterns: homogeneous (often SLE), speckled (many autoimmune diseases), centromere (limited scleroderma), nucleolar (systemic sclerosis), dense fine speckled (often no autoimmune disease).
When ordered
Joint pain with swelling, facial rash after sun exposure, mouth ulcers, fingers turning white or blue in cold, dry eyes and mouth. Usually not needed for tiredness or body ache alone.
Positive is not always disease
About 14% of people aged 12+ were ANA positive in a large US survey (NHANES 1999–2004). Infections and some medicines can also cause it. If positive, the doctor may order anti-dsDNA or an ENA profile. Usually no fasting; tell your doctor about medicines; one report alone is not a diagnosis.
